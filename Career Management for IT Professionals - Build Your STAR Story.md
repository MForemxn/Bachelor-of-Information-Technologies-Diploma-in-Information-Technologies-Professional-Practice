# Give an example of a time when you managed competing tasks and deadlines. What strategies did you use to complete your work?
> Consider the following interview question. Develop your answer using the STAR + L approach

## Situation
One of our major clients had their primary server crash unexpectedly. This outage brought down their domain controller, Exchange server, and network file share, effectively stopping staff from accessing email, shared drives, and [[authentication]] services. The incident caused significant disruption to their daily operations.

## Task
My responsibility was to restore core services as quickly as possible to minimize downtime, while also keeping the client informed and managing their expectations under pressure.

## Action
I began by performing a rapid root cause assessment to confirm whether the issue was hardware or software-related. Once I identified the failure, I immediately initiated the recovery plan, prioritising the domain controller to restore [[authentication]] services first. After stabilising Active Directory, I moved on to recovering the Exchange server, ensuring staff could [[resume]] email communication. Finally, I restored the network file shares from backup so employees could access their documents. Throughout the [[process]], I kept the client’s management updated at regular intervals, explained the steps being taken, and gave realistic timelines for service restoration. I also documented each stage of the recovery for post-incident [[review]].

## Result
Core IT services were restored within the same day, and staff were able to [[resume]] work with minimal [[data]] loss. The client commended both the speed of resolution and the clear communication during a stressful situation. Following the incident, I worked with them to implement stronger backup and redundancy measures to reduce risk of recurrence.

## Learning
I learned that in critical incidents, prioritisation and clear communication are just as important as technical troubleshooting. Restoring services in a structured order ([[authentication]] → communication → file access) ensured the client could get back to work quickly. This experience reinforced the importance of proactive disaster recovery planning, and I’ve since been more proactive in recommending resilience improvements to clients.


## Brief Version
One of my clients had their main server crash, which took down their domain controller, Exchange, and file shares. I quickly assessed the issue and prioritized restoring services in order of impact — first bringing the domain controller back online, then Exchange for email, and finally restoring file shares from backup. Throughout the [[process]], I kept the client updated with clear timelines so they knew what to expect. By the end of the day, all core services were restored with minimal [[data]] loss. Afterwards, I worked with them to improve their backup and redundancy strategy. That experience reinforced for me the importance of prioritisation and proactive communication when managing critical incidents.

# You have a code due this afternoon and are finding that you are having issues. Explain the steps you would take to diagnose and solve this issue.
> Consider the following interview question. Develop your answer using the STAR + L approach

## Situation
If I had a coding task due in the afternoon and discovered that my code wasn’t working as expected, I’d be aware that time was short and that I needed a clear, structured approach to resolve the problem.

## Task
My goal would be to gather as much information as possible about the cause of the problem first. I know this seems counter-intuitive given the idea of a short time period, but in my experience the more information you have about an issue the less pressure you feel and the more logical your thoughts can be. This would help me better find the cause of the issue, implement a fix quickly, and still ensure the final code was functional, tested, and submitted on time.

## Action
I’d begin by reproducing the issue consistently, so I understood when and where it occurred. I’d then review error logs, debug output, and any recent changes I’d made to pinpoint likely causes. Using debugging tools, I’d isolate the specific part of the code that was failing. If I couldn’t resolve it immediately, I’d look up documentation or search for similar examples online to guide my troubleshooting. If it became clear that I needed support, I’d escalate early — for example, asking a teammate or supervisor for input — rather than risk missing the deadline. Finally, once the bug was fixed, I’d run multiple test cases to confirm stability before submitting.

## Result
By applying a systematic approach, I’d expect to resolve the issue within the timeframe and deliver working code. If the ideal solution wasn’t achievable before the deadline, I’d implement a temporary fix or workaround so I could still submit functioning code, then return later for a more permanent solution.

## Learning
This process reinforces the importance of staying calm under pressure, breaking down complex problems into smaller parts, and communicating early if deadlines may be impacted — principles I already use daily in IT support when troubleshooting client systems.

## Brief Version
If I had a coding task due this afternoon and ran into issues, I’d stay calm and work through it systematically. First, I’d reproduce the error and check logs or recent changes to narrow down the cause. Then I’d use debugging tools and testing to isolate and fix the problem. If I couldn’t resolve it quickly, I’d research solutions or escalate early for support. My aim would be to deliver working code on time, even if that meant applying a temporary fix, and then review it later for a long-term solution. The key for me would be structured problem-solving and clear communication under pressure.
