# Wireless Communications - Chapter 1 Notes

## Overview

**Wireless communications**: Transmission of digital [[data]] without wires, encompassing various technologies from short-range [[Bluetooth]] to [[satellite]] networks.

## Key Wireless Technologies

### Short-Range Technologies

- **[[Bluetooth]]**: Very short range (few inches to 33 feet), eliminates cables between devices, uses low-power radio modules
- **WirelessHD/WiGig**: 7-10 Gbps speeds, up to 10m range, uses Ultra Wide Band (UWB)
- **Piconet/WPAN**: [[Bluetooth]] network of up to 7 devices

### Medium-Range Technologies

- **WLAN (802.11n)**: Up to 600 Mbps, 375 feet range, uses access points and wireless NICs
- **Fixed Broadband Wireless/WIMAX (802.16)**: Up to 25 miles coverage, 75 Mbps at 4 miles, 17-50 Mbps beyond 6 miles

### Long-Range Technologies

- **[[Cellular]] Networks**:
    - 4G: 100% digital, 150 Mbps stationary/45 Mbps pedestrian/20 Mbps vehicle
    - 3G: Up to 21 Mbps theoretical, 3-11 Mbps realistic
- **[[Satellite]]**: Very long distance, up to 250ms [[transmission delay]], uses repeaters

## Applications

### Industry Applications

- **Education**: Campus-wide connectivity, cost-effective classroom transformation
- **Healthcare**: Point-of-care systems, [[RFID]] patient tracking, medication verification
- **Government/Military**: Field [[data]] collection, digital battlefield systems, municipal services
- **Business**: Mobile workforce, disaster recovery, office [[flexibility]]

### Consumer Applications

- **[[Home]] Entertainment**: Wireless media distribution, smart [[home]] controls (ZigBee/802.15.4)
- **Travel**: GPS navigation, [[satellite]] radio, airport/airplane connectivity
- **Industrial**: Remote sensors (motes), equipment monitoring, warehouse management

## Advantages vs Disadvantages

### Advantages

- **Mobility**: Untethered workforce, organizational [[flexibility]]
- **Installation**: Easier/cheaper than cabling, especially in older buildings
- **Reliability**: Eliminates cable failure points
- **Disaster Recovery**: Quick relocation capability

### Disadvantages

- **Interference**: Radio signal conflicts require source [[identification]]/elimination
- **[[Security]]**: Potential for signal interception, requires access controls
- **Health Concerns**: RF radiation effects still under scientific study

## Technical Concepts

- **Digital Convergence**: Integration of voice, video, text processing in single devices
- **WWAN**: Corporate [[data]] access via [[cellular]] technologies
- **WAP2**: Standard for mobile web browsing on [[cellular]] devices
- **[[RFID]]**: Radio frequency [[identification]] for tracking/inventory

Network speeds vary based on: user count, traffic volume, interference levels, and environmental conditions.

# 1. Basics of Wireless Signals

Wireless [[data]] signals are transmitted via **electromagnetic waves**, traveling at the speed of light (**300,000 km/s**). Two primary types of electromagnetic waves are used:

- **Light Waves**: Typically infrared or visible light (e.g., LED-based systems).  
  - **Infrared Light**: Invisible to the human eye, less susceptible to interference. Used in applications like remote controls.  
  - *Diagram*: [Infrared Light Spectrum](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3c/EM_Spectrum_Properties_edit.svg/800px-EM_Spectrum_Properties_edit.svg.png)  
- **Radio Waves**: Generated by an electric current in a wire, creating a magnetic field that radiates outward.  
  - **Advantages**:  
    - Travel long distances.  
    - Penetrate nonmetallic objects.  
    - Invisible.  
  - *Diagram*: [Radio Wave Propagation](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4e/Radio_wave_propagation_line_of_sight.svg/800px-Radio_wave_propagation_line_of_sight.svg.png)

**Frequency**: Number of cycles per second (Hertz, Hz).  
- **Low Frequency**: Longer wavelengths, better penetration, lower [[data]] rates.  
- **High Frequency**: Shorter wavelengths, higher [[data]] rates, more susceptible to obstacles.  
- **Carrier Signal**: A continuous oscillating sine wave used to carry [[data]] via modulation.  
  - *Diagram*: [Sine Wave Carrier Signal](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/60/Sine_curve_drawing_animation.gif/800px-Sine_curve_drawing_animation.gif)

## 2. Analog & Digital Signals

### Analog Signals
- **Definition**: Continuous variation in intensity (voltage or amplitude).  
- **Characteristics**: Broadcast continuously without discrete breaks.  
- **Examples**: Audio, video, voice, light.  
- **Challenges**: Requires conversion to digital for computer processing.  
- *Diagram*: [Analog Signal Waveform](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2b/Analog_signal_example.png/800px-Analog_signal_example.png)

### Digital Signals
- **Definition**: Discrete pulses representing binary [[data]] (1 or 0).  
- **Characteristics**: Contains starts and stops in the signal stream.  
- **Examples**: Morse code, computer [[data]].  
- **Advantages**: Efficient for digital systems, easier to [[process]] and store.  
- *Diagram*: [Digital Signal Waveform](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7e/Digital_signal_example.png/800px-Digital_signal_example.png)

### Modem (Modulator/Demodulator)
- **Modulation**: Encodes digital [[data]] onto an analog carrier wave.  
- **Demodulation**: Extracts [[data]] from the analog wave at the receiver.  
- **Purpose**: Facilitates digital [[data]] transmission over analog mediums (e.g., air, phone lines).  
- *Diagram*: [Modem Modulation [[Process]]](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5b/Modem_diagram.svg/800px-Modem_diagram.svg.png)

### Transmission Speed
- **Bits per Second (bps)**: Measures [[data]] transmission speed.  
- **Baud Rate**: Number of signal units per second. A baud can represent multiple bits depending on modulation.  
- *Diagram*: [Baud Rate vs. Bit Rate](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3b/Bit_rate_vs_baud_rate.svg/800px-Bit_rate_vs_baud_rate.svg.png)

## 3. Analog Modulation

Analog modulation encodes analog information onto an analog carrier wave. The three main types are:

### Amplitude Modulation (AM)
- **Definition**: Varies the amplitude of the carrier wave to encode [[data]].  
- **Use Case**: AM radio broadcasting.  
- **Disadvantage**: Highly susceptible to interference.  
- *Diagram*: [Amplitude Modulation](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a4/AM_modulation.png/800px-AM_modulation.png)

### Frequency Modulation (FM)
- **Definition**: Varies the frequency of the carrier wave to encode [[data]].  
- **Advantages**: Less susceptible to interference, supports high-fidelity and stereophonic signals.  
- **Use Case**: FM radio broadcasting.  
- *Diagram*: [Frequency Modulation](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bf/FM_modulation.png/800px-FM_modulation.png)

### Phase Modulation (PM)
- **Definition**: Varies the phase of the carrier wave to encode [[data]].  
- **Advantages**: More reliable in noisy environments.  
- *Diagram*: [Phase Modulation](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0e/Phase_modulation_example.png/800px-Phase_modulation_example.png)

## 4. Digital Modulation

Digital modulation encodes binary [[data]] (1s and 0s) onto an analog carrier wave for transmission over analog mediums. The three main types are:

### Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK)
- **Definition**: Changes the amplitude of the carrier wave to represent binary 1 or 0.  
- **Characteristics**: Simple but noise-sensitive.  
- *Diagram*: [Amplitude Shift Keying](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8e/ASK_modulation.png/800px-ASK_modulation.png)

### Frequency Shift Keying (FSK)
- **Definition**: Changes the frequency of the carrier wave to represent binary 1 or 0.  
- **Characteristics**: More robust than ASK, requires more bandwidth.  
- *Diagram*: [Frequency Shift Keying](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6e/FSK_modulation.png/800px-FSK_modulation.png)

### Phase Shift Keying (PSK)
- **Definition**: Changes the phase of the carrier wave to represent binary [[data]].  
- **Advantages**: Highly reliable, as phase changes are easier to detect in noisy environments.  
- *Diagram*: [Phase Shift Keying](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7b/PSK_modulation.png/800px-PSK_modulation.png)

### Advantages of Digital Modulation
- Efficient bandwidth utilization.  
- Lower power requirements.  
- Robust against interference.  
- Compatible with digital systems via error-correcting techniques.

## 5. Spread Spectrum

Spread spectrum techniques distribute a signal over a wider frequency band to reduce interference and errors.

### Narrow-Band Transmissions
- Use a narrow frequency range.  
- Vulnerable to interference.  
- *Diagram*: [Narrow-Band vs. Spread Spectrum](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1a/Narrowband_vs_spread_spectrum.svg/800px-Narrowband_vs_spread_spectrum.svg.png)

### Spread Spectrum Techniques
- **Purpose**: Spreads a narrow-band signal over a broader frequency range, reducing interference and improving reliability.  
- **Types**:  
  - **Frequency Hopping Spread Spectrum (FHSS)**:  
    - Rapidly switches frequencies based on a pseudorandom hopping code.  
    - Multiple devices can operate in the same area using different hopping sequences.  
    - Interference affects only a small portion of the message.  
    - *Diagram*: [Frequency Hopping](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7e/FHSS_diagram.png/800px-FHSS_diagram.png)  
  - **Direct Sequence Spread Spectrum (DSSS)**:  
    - Uses a redundant chipping code to spread each [[data]] bit across multiple chips.  
    - Modulated using techniques like Quadrature Phase Shift Keying (QPSK).  
    - Appears as low-powered noise to unintended receivers, enhancing [[security]].  
    - Robust against interference due to statistical recovery techniques.  
    - More expensive than FHSS.  
    - *Diagram*: [Direct Sequence Spread Spectrum](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2c/DSSS_diagram.png/800px-DSSS_diagram.png)

## 6. Summary
- **Infrared vs. Radio Waves**: Infrared is invisible and less prone to interference; radio waves travel further and penetrate nonmetallic objects.  
- **Modulation/Demodulation**: Critical for encoding/decoding [[data]] over analog mediums.  
- **Analog vs. Digital Modulation**: Analog uses continuous changes; digital uses discrete binary changes for efficiency and reliability.  
- **Spread Spectrum**: FHSS and DSSS reduce interference and enhance [[security]] by spreading signals over a wider frequency band.
